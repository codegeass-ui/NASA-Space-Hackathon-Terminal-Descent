Act I • Engineering for the Deep Dark

The 175-Year Alignment & Twin Hardware

Even though Voyager 2 lifted off two weeks earlier, Voyager 1 took a faster trajectory and overtook its twin on December 15, 1977 to reach Jupiter first.

Built Where Sunlight Fades

Beyond Mars, sunlight is too faint for solar arrays. NASA engineered both probes with self-contained nuclear power and long-range communications designed to endure decades in deep space.

  • 3 MHW-RTG Nuclear Batteries Converted heat from decaying plutonium-238 into 470 watts of electricity at launch, losing roughly 4 watts each year.
  • 12-Foot (3.7 m) High-Gain Dish Antenna Focused faint radio whispers back to NASA’s Deep Space Network across billions of miles.
  • Imaging Science Subsystem (Cameras) Narrow- and wide-angle cameras that revealed active alien worlds before being shut off forever in 1990.

Fading Power & Retiring Instruments

To keep the spacecraft alive as power declines, engineers have turned off cameras and heaters one by one—mirroring the silent rovers resting on the Moon and Mars.

Act II • Planetary Discoveries

The Grand Tour of the Outer Giants

Using each planet’s gravity like a cosmic slingshot, Voyager 1 and 2 transformed distant points of light into vivid, dynamic worlds.

1979

Jupiter

Voyager 1 & 2

Revealed intricate turbulence inside the Great Red Spot and discovered active volcanoes erupting on moon Io—the first seen beyond Earth.

1980–81

Saturn

Voyager 1 & 2

Showed that Saturn’s rings consist of thousands of icy ringlets and uncovered a dense nitrogen atmosphere shrouding giant moon Titan.

1986

Uranus

Voyager 2 Solo

First and only spacecraft to visit the sideways-tilted ice giant, discovering 11 new moons, two faint rings, and a tilted magnetic field.

1989

Neptune

Voyager 2 Solo

Uncovered supersonic winds, the Great Dark Spot storm, and nitrogen ice geysers erupting on freezing moon Triton.

Act III • One Last Look at Home

The Pale Blue Dot

In February 1990, NASA had to permanently power down Voyager 1’s cameras to conserve electricity for its long journey into interstellar space. Before the spacecraft went blind forever, astronomer Carl Sagan asked mission leaders to turn the camera back toward Earth for one final portrait.

“From nearly four billion miles away, Earth appeared inside a single ray of sunlight—smaller than a single pixel. Everyone you have ever loved, every story ever told, all living on that tiny pale blue dot.”

Moments after transmitting that frame, Voyager 1’s imaging system was switched off for good—marking the transition from planetary photographer to silent interstellar ambassador.

Act IV • Humanity’s Time Capsule

The Voyager Golden Record

Bolted to the side of both spacecraft is a 12-inch gold-plated copper phonograph disk—complete with a stylus and engraved pulsar star-map instructions so any cosmic finder can play it and trace it back to Earth.

12-Inch Gold-Plated Copper Disk • Official Cover Diagram • NASA / JPL

Universal Science & Pulsar Map

The aluminum protective cover is engraved with diagrams explaining how to play the record using the enclosed cartridge and needle, alongside a map showing Earth’s exact position relative to 14 pulsing stars (pulsars) and the fundamental transition of the hydrogen atom.
